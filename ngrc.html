Drag the blue motor, then kick it. A soft coupling makes the hidden load lag, overshoot and ring. But this is a real machine, not a textbook: the coupling has backlash and a hardening spring, both masses have stiction that breaks away into sliding friction, the motor has cogging ripple, and the encoder quantises. Nobody has a model for any of that. The competitor is a Kalman filter — optimal for a linear plant — run twice: once handed the exact linear terms (masses, stiffness, damping) and once with the model an engineer actually has (masses and stiffness off the datasheet, no friction or damping at all). Both are tuned, so neither is handicapped, and neither can represent the nonlinearities — which is the whole point. NGRC is told nothing and learns the plant from the measurements alone. Everyone also reads the coupling force from a load cell on the shaft; on a linear spring that nearly gives the answer away (x₂ ≈ x₁ − f/k), so the one-line algebra is reported as a baseline too — the hardening spring is what breaks it. Amber caret = NGRC · green caret = exact-linear Kalman · block = the hidden truth. The caret glued to the block is winning; the error meters keep score. And a violet hollow caret is the preview: a direct 1 s-ahead readout predicting where the load will be — scored against the Kalman filter's own 1 s forecast, which cannot know the operator's future force either. The sensor trains until you lock it: after calibration it adapts toward truth, and once it's minimally ready the 🔒 button unlocks — drag and kick through a full excitation first (or don't), then switch to estimation and see what showing it more of the dynamics was worth. The error meters restart at each switch, so the scores judge the current mode only.

Draw loops and figure-8s for a few seconds. Two ghosts predict your fingertip 0.2–20 s ahead (set by the ghost horizon slider, default 10 s): gray = analogue k-NN (Lorenz's method of analogues: it finds the nearest past moment of your own drawing and bets history repeats — a real rival, not a strawman), cyan = ESN (an echo state network — the classical recurrent neural network NG-RC was designed to replace: 100 recurrent neurons, readouts trained online on the SAME targets), magenta = MLP (a plain feedforward network — the NN everyone learns first: one hidden layer on a 2-lag input window, trained online with Adam + experience replay on the SAME targets — your reference point if you know ML but not reservoir computing), green = raw AFM (the autopilot brain free-running from your last strokes — the NGRC-family attractor model without the shape-lock scaffolding) vs amber = NGRC, which learns a model of your motion — using the library's direct multi-horizon readouts, because iterating a 1-step model compounds its error (the chart shows all of them). The score is the path miss — how far each ghost lands from where your finger actually went. Then the showpiece — tap Autopilot: NGRC keeps drawing your doodle by itself (the library's AFM universal feature map, ReLU + Fourier, noise-hardened so your doodle becomes a true attractor) — and the rivals race it live: the analogue replays its recording, the ESN free-runs its own learned dynamics, and a no-learning straight-line just leaves the screen. Keep doodling ~10 s and the ghost locks onto your path: it tracks your phase around the doodle and learns your tempo profile (you slow at the same corners every lap), then predicts by riding the path ahead — the on-path advantage scores that against the analogue's replay.

Experiment summary — live methods + results, written for papers and chats

A servo axis shuttles an open tank of liquid between two stations. The wave left behind after each move is what limits cycle time on filling lines, ladle transfer and wet benches. The textbook fix is input shaping — but the slosh resonance depends on the fill depth, which moves it 1.8× across the range, far outside the ±20% a robust shaper tolerates. And the only instrument is one gauge pointing straight down at one spot, reading mean depth plus the local wave with no way to separate them from a single sample. Six identical machines run the same command, each differing from the one above by exactly one thing: red = control (no anti-slosh at all — the raw move); gray = conventional (shaper and feedforward mass both fixed at the nominal fill — what ships today); violet = hybrid, the retrofit — that controller completely untouched with a learned trim added on top, for when you cannot recertify the machine; green = parametric — the conventional structure kept exactly (same three smooth terms, same shaper) with its constants identified online instead of frozen at a nominal fill; magenta = super hybrid — both of those at once (identified constants and the learned trim) on that same untouched structure; and amber = experimental, which reads fill and resonance off that same single gauge and retunes both. Move the Fill slider: the gray tank starts ringing, the amber one settles back down. The hybrid answers what a plant actually asks — how much do you get without touching the controller? — and the measured answer is two-sided: with the shaper correctly tuned the trim cuts the wave by a third, and with it mistuned the trim buys almost nothing, because a mistuned shaper fails on timing and a force correction cannot re-time anything. The magenta machine answers the obvious follow-up — are those two corrections redundant? — and the measured answer is no: identifying the constants cannot produce a term the structure does not contain, so the trim still has systematic residue to absorb, and stacking them beats either alone (most of all at fills far from where the one-time calibration was taken). Then inject a fault — amber cross-checks three independent routes to the same physical quantity and names what broke, while the conventional following-error alarm sees nothing, because the position loop absorbs the fault into control effort. Health check ▶ runs a deliberate unshaped probe: a well-controlled machine leaves no wave to measure, so diagnosis has to ask for one.
