④ Architecture

A hybrid plant: the gearbox is a lumped nonlinear element and the link is a lattice of elastic material, stepped together. The encoder sits on the motor side of the gear teeth, so it is structurally blind to wind-up and to the link's own bending — which is why a robot's accuracy and its repeatability are different numbers.

What the picture shows

The dashed line is where the program asked the tool to be. The pale line is where the encoder says it is — the controller's own picture, and it is wrong by the wind-up. The solid amber curve is the truth: the link at its real angle, bent by its own load. Deflections are drawn exaggerated by the slider above (they are well under a percent of the arm), and the exaggeration factor is printed on the stage so the picture can never be read as scale.

Soft sensor — the tip from motor signals alone

The encoder cannot see the tip. This learns the tip error from signals a real controller already has — commanded torque, encoder speed and acceleration, and the pose as sin/cos — while the tracker is still on the machine, and then locks. Everything scored below is what the machine would produce for the rest of its life; a model still adapting is being told the answer.

What it estimates, and against what

The target is the tip's offset from where the encoder says it is — the part of the error the controller is structurally blind to. It differs from the error against the commanded angle (the amber trace above) by exactly the following error, which the controller does know: tipError = trackingError − L·(encoder − commanded). Scores are nRMSE against the truth's own spread, so 1.0 is no better than predicting the mean — which is precisely what "the tip is where the encoder says" scores, because the error averages to nearly zero while never being zero. The forecast is a second target of the same readout, 150 steps ahead: what a compensator needs, since a correction takes effect after the loop closes.

Two errors, two mechanisms, one fix each

Compensation pre-distorts the commanded angle by the deflection its identified compliance predicts, so it removes the bias and does nothing to the ringing. Input shaping convolves the reference with impulses summing to one, so it cancels the oscillation and — since it cannot change where a move ends — leaves the bias untouched. Turn each on alone and watch one number move and the other stand still. Invert the correction is there because a compensator applied backwards does not merely fail: it applies the error a second time, and the bias doubles.

Two joints, two lattice links, one coupled solve. A chain is not two single-joint arms next to each other: the inertia the shoulder must accelerate depends on the elbow angle, and accelerating either joint loads the other through the mass matrix's off-diagonal. Command the shoulder alone and watch the elbow's gearbox wind up anyway — the encoder on joint 2 sees none of it.

Tool sensor — per-joint or whole-arm?

A chain forces a choice a single joint does not: does each axis estimate from its own signals (what a distributed drive naturally supports, and what every servo vendor's compensation package looks like) or does one model read every axis? Both are trained here on the same stream, at the same 544-feature capacity — the whole-arm model gets 3 lags of 10 signals, the elbow-only one 6 lags of 5, so the only difference is which signals, and the single-axis model gets the longer window.

And which one wins depends on the regime — measured, not asserted

Measured offline at 1200 training pairs — with both joints moving the whole-arm model wins, nRMSE 0.069 against 0.177. With the elbow commanded to hold it loses: 0.562 against 0.494 — and both models are five to eight times worse there, so that regime is harder for everyone, not just for one architecture. I do not have a clean mechanism for the reversal, and the obvious one does not survive measurement: matching the two models' time span as well as their feature count (3 lags at stride 5 against 6 at stride 2) makes the whole-arm model worse still, 0.793. Switch the selector above and watch it happen. This page trains fewer pairs than the offline runs, so its live numbers sit higher than both rows.

Why the command wanders

The move's amplitude is modulated at an incommensurate rate, so no two cycles are alike. A 544-feature model fitted to an exactly periodic stream can score beautifully by learning where in the cycle it is — and then the test set is the same cycle it trained on and the number means nothing. Measured offline: 0.0142 with the modulation off, 0.0454 with it on, and a window taken further out still — states even less like the training ones — reads 0.0355, lower rather than higher. That is what says it generalises rather than recalls.

The chart is the claim

The elbow is commanded to hold still in "shoulder only" mode, and its gearbox transmits a torque anyway. The solid trace is what it carries; the two dotted ones split its inertial load into M₂₁·α₁ — what the shoulder's acceleration puts on it — and M₂₂·α₂, its own. The first dominates. The constant offset between the solid trace and the dotted pair is gravity holding the forearm up, which the controller's model already knows; what it does not know is the coupling, and that is the part that moves the tool.

Plotting the sum against the solid trace would prove nothing — M₂₁α₁ + M₂₂α₂ ≡ τ₂ + G₂ − C₂ is the equation of motion itself. Splitting it is what says something.

Fold the elbow and the shoulder changes machine

M₁₁ carries 2·m₂·L₁·c₂·cos q₂, so the inertia the shoulder must accelerate changes by about a factor of two across the elbow's range. A controller tuned at one pose is mistuned at another — which is why the servo here is computed torque, evaluating the arm's own rigid model at the commanded pose rather than carrying fixed per-joint gains. What it deliberately does not model is the gearbox compliance or either link's flexibility: that is the whole subject of this tab.

Why hybrid

Joint compliance — gearbox torsional stiffness, bearing compliance, harmonic-drive lost motion — is commonly cited at 70–90% of an industrial arm's tip deflection, and it is inherently zero-dimensional: a torque in, an angle out. Resolving it on a voxel lattice would be resolving the wrong term at great cost. So joints are lumped and links are the lattice, which is what buys real mode shapes, stiffness that depends on where the load sits, and sections a beam element cannot express. The split is measured rather than inherited: at this page's defaults the link carries most of it, because the gearbox here is stiff.

One lattice per link, not one sparse lattice

Three links of 0.6/0.5/0.3 m at 8 mm cells are ~22k cells of material inside a ~2.3M-cell swept bounding box — a fill fraction near 1%, and ~240 MiB against ~2.3 MiB, which is past the 128 MiB storage-binding limit most devices allow. Each link is a rigid-body transform of its own geometry, so each gets its own small dense lattice in its own body frame with the joint rotation carried as a transform. That also removes the need for a co-rotational formulation: a link rotating rigidly is stationary in its own frame, so there is no large rotation inside any lattice. The price is that the body frame is non-inertial, so centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler forces are not optional — they are local per-cell body forces, structurally identical to gravity.

Verified, not asserted

P and S wave speeds to 0.04% with second-order convergence; a solid at rest stays exactly at rest; uniaxial stress with free lateral faces recovers E and ν to machine precision; the cantilever against Timoshenko with second-order convergence; gravity's linear σ(x) to 8e-9 % and a rotating bar's quadratic σ(r) to 0.04%; the gearbox resonance to 0.2%; and the encoder reading the link angle plus exactly the wind-up, to 1e-9 relative.
