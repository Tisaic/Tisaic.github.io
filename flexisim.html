⑥ Architecture

A hybrid plant: the gearbox is a lumped nonlinear element and the link is a lattice of elastic material, stepped together. The encoder sits on the motor side of the gear teeth, so it is structurally blind to wind-up and to the link's own bending — which is why a robot's accuracy and its repeatability are different numbers.

What the picture shows — five positions of one arm

desired (grey dashed) is where the program asked the tool to be, and it is the reference everything else is drawn against. commanded motor (violet) is desired plus the pre-distortion — with no correction the two coincide, and with one the violet line deliberately leaves the target. actual motor (pale) is the encoder: the controller's entire picture of the machine, and it is blind to everything past the gear teeth. true arm (amber) is the link at its real angle, bent by its own load. estimated tip (cyan ring) is what the soft sensor thinks, once it has one.

Every deviation is drawn at the same magnification. That sounds obvious and the first version did not do it: it magnified the link's bending by the slider and drew the gearbox wind-up at 1×, so the two halves of one error were on two scales and the bending outvoted the wind-up by exactly the magnification. Measured over one move, that put the true tool on the wrong side of the encoder for 3.2% of it — reported from a phone as an arm flexing in a way that is not possible. The plant was right throughout: the tip flexes further down as an upward move accelerates (bend −0.121 → −0.269 over the first 300 steps), which is what inertia must do. Deviations are drawn as transverse offsets rather than as rotations, because a magnified rotation would change the arm's length on screen — a second lie.

Soft sensor — the tip from motor signals alone

The encoder cannot see the tip. This learns the tip error from signals a real controller already has — commanded torque, encoder speed and acceleration, and the pose as sin/cos — while the tracker is still on the machine, and then locks. Everything scored below is what the machine would produce for the rest of its life; a model still adapting is being told the answer.

What it estimates, and against what

The target is the tip's offset from where the encoder says it is — the part of the error the controller is structurally blind to. It differs from the error against the commanded angle (the amber trace above) by exactly the following error, which the controller does know: tipError = trackingError − L·(encoder − commanded). Scores are nRMSE against the truth's own spread, so 1.0 is no better than predicting the mean — which is precisely what "the tip is where the encoder says" scores, because the error averages to nearly zero while never being zero. The forecast is a second target of the same readout, 150 steps ahead: what a compensator needs, since a correction takes effect after the loop closes.

Three corrections, and what each is allowed to know

① Open loop commands the trajectory and nothing else: the tool lands wherever the wind-up and the sag put it, and the encoder reports none of it. ② Open loop + prediction pre-distorts the command by the deflection the identified compliance predicts for it — evaluated at the command, never at anything measured, so it needs no sensor at run time and is only as right as the constant behind it. ③ Closed loop throws the model away and drives the estimated tip onto the setpoint using the soft sensor alone; it is a lag with a time constant of two bending periods, so it converges over several moves rather than acting on the next step.

Two errors, two mechanisms, one fix each

Every correction here removes the bias and does nothing to the ringing. Input shaping convolves the reference with impulses summing to one, so it cancels the oscillation and — since it cannot change where a move ends — leaves the bias untouched. That is why the table scores them separately: as a single rms, compensation reads as a disappointment. Invert the correction is there because a compensator applied backwards does not merely fail: it applies the error a second time, and the bias doubles.

Why commissioning deliberately wobbles the arm

While the sensor is training, a small dither is added to the commanded angle — the pre-distortion mode ③ will later apply, excited on purpose. Without it the model has only ever seen the machine uncorrected, so the first thing the closed loop does is move the encoder somewhere the model has never been, and a fit asked about an operating point it never saw answers confidently and wrong — measured, the estimate reaches 26× the truth and the loop runs away in positive feedback. With the dither it is flat across the range the loop occupies and the loop converges. It costs about 8% of the locked open-loop accuracy, which is the price of a loop that converges rather than diverges.

Two joints, two lattice links, one coupled solve. A chain is not two single-joint arms next to each other: the inertia the shoulder must accelerate depends on the elbow angle, and accelerating either joint loads the other through the mass matrix's off-diagonal. Command the shoulder alone and watch the elbow's gearbox wind up anyway — the encoder on joint 2 sees none of it.

Correction — applied at the shoulder, levered by the reach's projection

The rigid model is given M(q), the Coriolis terms, gravity, both stiffnesses and both lever arms, and it is evaluated one servo time constant AHEAD. The closed loop is given nothing but the estimate, and only once it is locked. ①–③ are all quasi-static — one instant of the command in, one deflection out — so none of them can cancel a resonance, which is most of what is left. ④ is fitted over a window of the command and can: it is converged against the tracker on three repeatable moves and then deployed on this deliberately non-repeating one, which is its generalisation test rather than a convenience.

Tool sensor — per-joint or whole-arm?

A chain forces a choice a single joint does not: does each axis estimate from its own signals (what a distributed drive naturally supports, and what every servo vendor's compensation package looks like) or does one model read every axis? Both are trained here on the same stream, at matched capacity — the whole-arm model reads twice the signals and gets half the lags at twice the spacing, so both have the same base dimension AND the same window reach, and the only difference is which signals. The live counts are in the panel below rather than restated here.

And which one wins depends on the regime — and on the command — measured, not asserted

The Jerk limit slider changes the answer, which was not expected. Measured in Node, one script, one variable, both models on the same stream at matched capacity — nRMSE, and the absolute error behind it: with the jerk limit off, whole arm 0.0814 against elbow-only 0.1911 (2.35×, absolute 4.09e-2 / 9.60e-2); at the shipped 120, 0.1798 against 0.1821 — a tie (absolute 8.54e-2 / 8.63e-2). The truth's own spread barely moves (5.03e-1 → 4.75e-1), so this is not a normalisation artefact: the whole-arm model's absolute error doubles while the elbow-only one slightly improves. Its advantage lived in the shoulder's sharp acceleration steps — the coupling term M₂₁·α₁ is the one thing the elbow cannot see directly, and an unlimited trapezoid makes it a distinctive high-frequency signal. Smooth the command and that channel carries much less. Widening the whole-arm window does not recover it (0.2314 at stride 5). It is a real cost of a smoother machine, and it is stated rather than tuned away.

And which one wins depends on the regime too

Measured offline at 1200 training pairs — with both joints moving the whole-arm model wins, nRMSE 0.069 against 0.177. With the elbow commanded to hold it loses: 0.562 against 0.494 — and both models are five to eight times worse there, so that regime is harder for everyone, not just for one architecture. I do not have a clean mechanism for the reversal, and the obvious one does not survive measurement: matching the two models' time span as well as their feature count (3 lags at stride 5 against 6 at stride 2) makes the whole-arm model worse still, 0.793. Switch the selector above and watch it happen. This page trains fewer pairs than the offline runs, so its live numbers sit higher than both rows.

Why the command wanders

The move's amplitude is modulated at an incommensurate rate, so no two cycles are alike. A model with hundreds of features fitted to an exactly periodic stream can score beautifully by learning where in the cycle it is — and then the test set is the same cycle it trained on and the number means nothing. Measured offline: 0.0142 with the modulation off, 0.0454 with it on, and a window taken further out still — states even less like the training ones — reads 0.0355, lower rather than higher. That is what says it generalises rather than recalls.

The chart is the claim

The elbow is commanded to hold still in "shoulder only" mode, and its gearbox transmits a torque anyway. The solid trace is what it carries; the two dotted ones split its inertial load into M₂₁·α₁ — what the shoulder's acceleration puts on it — and M₂₂·α₂, its own. The first dominates. The constant offset between the solid trace and the dotted pair is gravity holding the forearm up, which the controller's model already knows; what it does not know is the coupling, and that is the part that moves the tool.

Plotting the sum against the solid trace would prove nothing — M₂₁α₁ + M₂₂α₂ ≡ τ₂ + G₂ − C₂ is the equation of motion itself. Splitting it is what says something.

Fold the elbow and the shoulder changes machine

M₁₁ carries 2·m₂·L₁·c₂·cos q₂, so the inertia the shoulder must accelerate changes by about a factor of two across the elbow's range. A controller tuned at one pose is mistuned at another — which is why the servo here is computed torque, evaluating the arm's own rigid model at the commanded pose rather than carrying fixed per-joint gains. What it deliberately does not model is the gearbox compliance or either link's flexibility: that is the whole subject of this tab.

This is the tab that matches the end application. The other tabs run point-to-point moves and score where the tool ends up; a contouring machine never ends up anywhere — it is always mid-path, and what it is judged on is the shape it leaves behind. The two questions have different answers, so they get different metrics and different controls.

The deviation from the program splits in two and only one half is a defect. Contour error is the component normal to the path: the part is the wrong shape and no amount of time fixes it. Lag is the component along it: the tool is on the right curve, just late, so the part is right and the cycle is slower. Summing them into one tracking number — which every point-to-point metric on this project does — makes a machine that lags uniformly look as bad as one that cuts the wrong shape.

Commission traces one lap with the tracker watching and fits one compliance constant per joint to the tool's own deviation, then puts the tracker away — the same lifecycle as every other learner here. Commission ⑥ goes further: it is never told the arm's lengths at all — it visits held points inside the command box while the tracker teaches it the geometry, fits the inverse map itself, then runs the pilot's noise commissioning on top of it. Cascade depth applies to both: at 2 or 3, a SECOND (and third) pilot is commissioned on the machine with the one below it deployed and frozen, so each layer models what the previous one left rather than repeating it — the cost is commissioning time multiplied, and each layer still has to vouch for itself on the machine or the stack stops there. Control rate is the pilot's own clock, in decisions per settling time: at 30 the gap between decisions is wider than the ~40 steps a corner's acceleration lasts, so the pilot cannot resolve the event it most needs to pre-shape. The effort weight rescales itself with the rate, which is why this is one slider and not two — moving the clock alone measures WORSE, and that is how the gain stayed hidden. Sweep runs the same path at every feedrate and tabulates what each one costs, because accuracy and energy do not have their optimum at the same speed.

⑤ is the flagship: route, limit, run, deploy

The pilot (lib/pilot/) is told nothing about this machine — not that it is an arm, not its lengths, not its gearboxes. The engineering is four verbs: route six measured signals in, two corrections out, a tracker during commissioning only, and the program's look-ahead at runtime; limit each channel's position box, velocity, acceleration and jerk, a correction cap, torque guards and a workspace predicate; run — it settles, probes each channel's response, builds a filtered-noise excitation verified against every limit on the commanded sequence itself, auto-tunes its windows and ridge on held-out data, and then puts the finished controller on the machine against doing nothing, interleaved; deploy only if that measured an improvement. Runtime is a warm-started box-QP over a ladder of forecast readouts — fixed iteration count, PLC-shaped.

Measured here, one commissioning (~2 minutes of machine time), then programs it has never seen: rounded rect contour 1.34e-1 → 2.18e-2 (6.2×) — below the 2.53e-2 that ④ needs fourteen laps of that exact part to reach — on 36% less copper than the open loop and 23 torque reversals against ④'s 34; circle 7.1e-2 → 1.0e-2 (7.0×). Three instruments got it there, each recorded in the history: an attribution instrument (the plan's own predicted residual against what the machine delivered — 2.16×, which convicted the execution model, not the forecast; two timing-registration fixes later it reads 1.04×), a horizon measurement (a plan that reaches only one settling time ahead delivers every correction ~90% complete — 1.5× the settle fixed it), and a pure-simulation null (the same QP against an exactly known plant leaves 2.7e-6 — the machinery was never the limit). What separates the circle's remaining 1.0e-2 from ④'s converged 8.9e-4 is uniform, not localised: the residual is the free error's own shape at ~10% scale, everywhere on the lap — nine model-side levers measured null against it, recorded in the history — because the correction changes the state path, and the state-dependent disturbance it was recorded against moves with it, by about a tenth of itself. Only a lap cut under the correction can measure that (fold one observed residual back in and the circle reads 8.2e-4, in a single pass): the structural line between one shot and iteration, measured rather than chased. A cutting load that makes ④ two to six times worse leaves the pilot still helping; the square's corners pin the correction at the engineer's own cap — an authority limit. Every number is in docs/history/flexisim.md.

⑥ removes the last thing the pilot was given: the kinematics. Commissioning visits held points inside the command box while the tracker teaches the geometry; the fitted inverse (180 points → 1.2e-4 rad holdout in Node) then turns Cartesian programs into channel commands itself, and the pilot commissions on top with its truth routed through that same map. Holding real path points the learned map beats the analytic ik() 23–44× — the drawing does not know the droop or the wind-up; the machine does. The full chain lands within 9% of the analytic system on the rectangle (2.37e-2 vs 2.18e-2). ⑦ adds iteration on top, mapping the lap's error through the learned routing too: measured in Node, circle 5.9e-2 → 1.1e-3 by lap 14 — within 28% of what the analytic-kinematics ILC reaches — and rectangle 6.97e-2 → 1.27e-2, twice below that ILC's converged 2.53e-2. A system that learned its own geometry, its own dynamics, and then let the part teach it the rest. ⑤+④ is the same stacking on the analytic chain — the pilot does the one-shot bulk and the table folds in what only a deployed lap can see (the boundary brick 38 measured): circle to 1.03e-3 and rectangle to 1.30e-2 by lap 14. Set the two stacks side by side and the finding is that iteration erases the difference between knowing the kinematics and having learned them: 1.03e-3 vs 1.14e-3, 1.30e-2 vs 1.27e-2.

What the picture is, exactly

The arm is drawn at its true geometry with no exaggeration anywhere — at this stiffness the links bend by well under a percent of their length, so a straight-looking arm is the honest picture. The error trail is the exaggerated one, and it is exaggerated in one direction only: each recorded tool sample is drawn at the nearest point of the commanded path, pushed out along that point's normal by the magnification times the contour error. So the trail is a picture of the contour error alone, wrapped around the shape that produced it, and the lag — which is not a defect — deliberately does not appear in it.

Slowing down runs out, and the sweep is where you watch it run out

Contour rms with no correction, straight off the Sweep button, uncorrected: 7.19e-1 at feed 1.6e-2, 5.61e-1, 3.50e-1, 1.34e-1 at 4e-3, 3.97e-2 at 2e-3, 3.03e-2 at 1e-3. Halving the feedrate from 4e-3 buys 3.4×; halving it again buys only 1.31×. It flattens because what is left down there is the arm sagging under its own weight and winding up its gearboxes against gravity, and neither of those cares how fast you go. That is the argument for correcting the error rather than creeping around the part.

The two energy numbers disagree about the answer, which is why both are shown

Over the same sweep, copper loss ∫τ² reads 6.62e-3 · 5.39e-3 · 1.95e-3 · 5.93e-4 · 1.258e-4 · 1.365e-4 from fast to slow — an interior minimum at feed 2e-3, because going fast costs acceleration and going slow costs holding the arm up for longer. Mechanical work ∫|τω| over the same rows falls monotonically (2.58e-1 → 1.18e-2) and says go as slow as you can. A page reporting one of them would be choosing which cost to care about without saying so. Torque direction changes agree with the work: 22 at the top of the ladder against 4 at the bottom.

A static compliance correction stops working, and it does not degrade gracefully

A compliance constant answers how far does this torque push the tool off, which is a quasi-static question — right only while the command's own frequency content sits below the structure's modes. Measured with the identified constants against none: 5.4× at feed 1e-3, 1.8× at 2e-3, 1.03× at 4e-3, and slightly worse at 1e-2. Above the modes the error is a ringing response with a phase of its own, and no constant times the present torque has a phase.

Two things about the constants themselves. They come out at 7.3× and 22.5× the gearbox's own 1/K, so on this arm the gearbox is not the dominant compliance — the links are, which is the opposite of the 70–90% split the Move tab measures at its own stiffnesses. And the identifying lap deliberately runs at the bottom of the feedrate ladder: fitted at the shipped feedrate instead, the elbow's constant came out with the wrong sign and the correction then doubled the contour error. This project has measured that before on a single joint — during a move the gearbox's damper carries most of the torque, so a static fit to dynamic data lands on a blend of a stiffness and a damping, which is neither.

Direction changes are counted as travel, not as sign changes

Each velocity reversal drags a joint back through its own lost motion and re-breaks stiction — the textbook cause of the quadrant glitch that shows up as a step at every axis crossing of a circular interpolation, and the most recognisable defect in CNC contouring. Counting sign changes of joint velocity measures arithmetic instead: a joint dwelling near zero crosses it on rounding alone, measured at 994 against one physical reversal. A reversal is counted only once the joint has actually retraced more than its backlash, which is when the gear teeth have changed faces.

Friction is where this method's premise stops holding, and the slider is here so that is visible rather than assumed. The whole architecture rests on the PLANT PATH being linear enough to invert while the DISTURBANCE is not — and every number on this tab was measured on a joint with friction switched off, which is the one configuration where that premise cannot fail. Where it fails is measured, and it is further up than this note used to claim. At Coulomb friction of 2% and 5% of the motor-side hold torque the identification is untroubled — R² 0.998 and 0.997, DC gain 12.9 and 14.9 against a true 15.5 — and the module designs and deploys as usual. At 20% it collapses: the step test returns a DC gain of −4.26, the probe implies −0.03, and the fit explains 0.25% of held-out data. A PRBS reverses sign every grid interval and re-breaks stiction each time, which the step test — crossing zero velocity once — never sees, so the two disagree and the module REFUSES rather than designing against it. Before the gate existed it deployed a correction and made the machine 0.81×, reported as 1.33× better.

The earlier version of this note said 2% and it was wrong by a factor of a hundred: it was measured while the ladder was a fraction of the load-side hold torque, and the note survived the correction to motor-side. A stale number in a note is the same defect as a stale number in a check, and the check next to it had gone stale in exactly the same way.

The jerk limit is a boxcar convolved into the command's acceleration, so it is the same object as an input shaper and composes with one. It changes what the machine is ASKED to do, which is a different question from what the controller does about it — so changing it throws the commissioning away and the module identifies the new command from scratch. Off is a bare trapezoid: acceleration steps, and the correction has to answer a transient that arrives all at once. At the far end the move is so smooth that most of what the correction was for has already been removed by the profile. Settle is the dwell added on top of what the command's own delays need, in periods of the link's ring: it is the time the MACHINE gets to stop moving before the next move starts, where the jerk limit changes how the move is commanded. It is the host's number — a motion program's dwell, sized from the CAD bending period — and the module is told neither it nor the period; a longer dwell is simply a different command, so it throws the commissioning away too.

One button, and every number in it comes from the machine: wait until it is quiet → one step to measure the timescale and the DC gain → a pseudo-random probe to identify the impulse response and the disturbance together → design the inverse against HELD-OUT data → correct → train the soft sensor → lock. It also predicts what it will achieve before it deploys anything, and backs off if the answer is that it cannot help.

What it is given, and what it works out

Given: ref(k) — one number, readable at any step, because a controller planned the trajectory; one scalar correction to add to it; five signals per sample; and the tracker while commissioning. Nothing else — not the units, not the geometry, not the sign convention, not even that the machine is a robot.
Worked out: the settling time (from a step, with "quiet" detected rather than counted), the DC gain and its sign — which on this arm comes back at about 14 against a real arm length of 15.5 it was never told — the impulse response and its delay, the bending mode from that response's own ringing (~960 steps against a real ~980), a nonlinear map from the command window to the disturbance, and the regularised inverse that cancels it.

Is it treating this as an arm, or as any system? — measured, on three

As any system, with one stated assumption. The identical module, unchanged apart from a sample rate, is driven in test/blackbox/ against three plants that share no physics: a lightly damped actuator, an over-damped process with a NEGATIVE gain two hundred times smaller and no ringing at all, and this arm. Measured — 0.98× / 2.19× / 1.15×, with the gains identified rather than given, spanning 200× and both signs. If a plant constant had leaked in, exactly one of them would work.
THE ASSUMPTION IS LINEARITY OF THE PLANT PATH — and it was tested rather than assumed. Running the identical trajectory twice, once with a multi-level probe and once without, and differencing, removes the disturbance exactly and leaves the probe's response alone. On this arm that response departs from exact linear scaling by 0.04% over a 4× amplitude range, and an LTI model explains 99.03% of it. Hammerstein (poly-3, and a deadzone basis), LPV scheduled on commanded velocity, and a backlash direction bit were all fitted to the same record and all scored worse out of sample (0.88–0.98× against LTI) — exactly as they do on a control plant that is linear by construction. The nonlinear-identification question has a clean negative answer here. A binary PRBS could not have asked it at all: it visits two amplitudes, so any static nonlinearity agreeing at ±a fits identically.

The price of knowing nothing

Tab ① reaches about 4.6× on this plant with the CAD in hand (open loop 1.03e-1 → learned filter 2.27e-2). This reaches 3.85× in the Node harness with none of it — so the price of knowing nothing about this plant is roughly 20%, not the factor of four it first appeared to be.
THAT FIRST NUMBER WAS A UNITS BUG, NOT A LIMIT, and it is worth knowing how it hid. The disturbance map's taps were sized in identification-grid samples and then used as solver steps, so its window spanned 70 steps instead of 2800 — a fiftieth of the move it was meant to explain. Every symptom pointed elsewhere: it explained 50% of the disturbance, so the design honestly predicted 1.4× and honestly achieved 1.07×, the identification was excellent, and the prediction matched the outcome. Nothing looked broken; it looked like the cost of ignorance. What found it was asking a different question — a crude 96-bin phase-indexed average explained 100% of the same disturbance, which proves it was perfectly predictable and therefore that the map was failing rather than the problem being hard.

Why hybrid

Joint compliance — gearbox torsional stiffness, bearing compliance, harmonic-drive lost motion — is commonly cited at 70–90% of an industrial arm's tip deflection, and it is inherently zero-dimensional: a torque in, an angle out. Resolving it on a voxel lattice would be resolving the wrong term at great cost. So joints are lumped and links are the lattice, which is what buys real mode shapes, stiffness that depends on where the load sits, and sections a beam element cannot express. The split is measured rather than inherited: at this page's defaults the link carries most of it, because the gearbox here is stiff.

One lattice per link, not one sparse lattice

Three links of 0.6/0.5/0.3 m at 8 mm cells are ~22k cells of material inside a ~2.3M-cell swept bounding box — a fill fraction near 1%, and ~240 MiB against ~2.3 MiB, which is past the 128 MiB storage-binding limit most devices allow. Each link is a rigid-body transform of its own geometry, so each gets its own small dense lattice in its own body frame with the joint rotation carried as a transform. That also removes the need for a co-rotational formulation: a link rotating rigidly is stationary in its own frame, so there is no large rotation inside any lattice. The price is that the body frame is non-inertial, so centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler forces are not optional — they are local per-cell body forces, structurally identical to gravity.

Point-to-point and contouring are different machines

Tabs ① and ② ask where the tool ends up: bias, oscillation, settling time, and the controls that serve those are input shaping and a settle dwell. Tab ③ asks what shape the tool leaves, and there the same two controls are a defect and a no-op respectively — a shaper works by delaying the command, which costs nothing at the end of a move and is a contour error everywhere along one, and a machine that never settles has no settled window to dwell for. The end application is contouring, so ③ is the tab that matches it; ① and ② remain the cheapest place to see the plant itself, which is why they are still here.
