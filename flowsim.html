A 3D lattice field engine: physical quantities live as distributed fields on a spatial lattice, and every update is local — read this cell, read its neighbours, apply a physics operator, write the next state. The first operator is a D3Q19 lattice Boltzmann fluid, which is where the density and velocity you see come from; they are moments of the distribution, not independently stored variables.

A sensor samples one lattice cell over time — and it is also the measured point the soft sensor below reads from. The chart reads that cell every frame — speed, the two in-plane velocity components, and density — which is how an oscillation is told from a steady flow: a vortex street shows as a periodic transverse velocity, and a settled flow as flat lines.

The sensor is the probe: the point you could actually instrument. The target is the point you could not — the soft sensor estimates it from the sensor's recent history alone, and the target's true value is used only to train and to grade. Switch to estimation freezes the model so you can see whether what it learned holds when it stops being told the answer. Every score is out of sample: each value is graded before the pair it came from is trained on, and the prediction is graded only once its target has actually arrived. Both are reported as error ÷ the truth's own standard deviation, so 1.0 means no better than predicting the average. The violet trace is drawn at the step it is about, not the step it was made, so it lies on top of the truth exactly when it came true.

Compressibility — a tunable equation of state. Standard LBM fixes the pressure at p = ρcs² with the lattice sound speed cs = 0.577, so a fast flow drives large density swings (the Ma² error that clamps at high speed). This adds a pressure force so the effective sound speed is a knob: a stiffer fluid is a lower Mach number at the same velocity, which is how you push the flow faster before compressibility bites. Non-ideal makes the stiffness rise with density — the foundation a multiphase model builds on. Verified against the analytic sound speed of a pressure wave (a stiffer fluid propagates sound faster, measured to <0.1%). D3Q19 isothermal LBM cannot do true shocks or supersonic flow — that needs a thermal scheme.

The inlet can be made to vary, which is the difference between a flow that settles and one that never does. A pulsing inlet is periodic, so "it will do what it did one period ago" still predicts it — that is the control. Quasi-periodic uses three tones at irrational frequency ratios, so the signal never repeats. Chaotic drives it from a Lorenz system, which has a continuous spectrum and is genuinely unpredictable beyond a horizon. Measured on the drive signals themselves, the autocorrelation after one nominal period falls 0.97 → 0.57 → 0.12, and a period-lookup baseline goes from 0.03 (near-perfect) to 1.23 (worse than predicting the average). That is the point: past the first mode, nothing a table can replay will predict this flow.

Drag on the 2D slice to stir the fluid. That applies a real body force in a sphere of lattice cells for a few dozen steps — momentum goes into the momentum field and is then transported by the same operator as everything else. Watch the residual row: it is how far the velocity field moves per step, so you can watch a run settle back to steady after you poke it rather than guessing from the picture. Per step, so it means the same thing whatever you set steps-per-frame to.

Field reconstruction (dye scene). A ring of wall sensors reads only velocity and pressure at the channel walls — never the dye — and one shared-reservoir NGRC readout per cell reconstructs the whole concentration slice from them. Turn it on to replace the true plume with what the sensors infer; the number is the field nRMSE (1.0 = no better than the mean). It is the industrial soft sensor: read cheap upstream signals, infer the composition you cannot instrument. Turn it on and a second pane appears: the true dye above, the reconstruction below, on the same colour range — two independently auto-scaled pictures cannot be compared, so the reconstruction is normalised against the truth and its error shows as real over- and under-shoot. The magenta marks are the sensors: everything the model is allowed to see. The number is the field nRMSE (1.0 = no better than the mean). Two modes, and the difference is the whole claim. While it says training the model is re-fitted on the true field every sample — the commissioning position, where the answer is always available. Go blind 🔒 freezes the weights and runs the readout from the wall sensors alone; the truth is then read only to grade. A soft sensor that is continuously handed the answer cannot demonstrate it could work without one, so the blind score is the one that means something.

Reading the numbers

Everything above is in lattice units, which is where LBM's stability conditions are stated: the relaxation time τ must stay above ½ (it is the viscosity — ν = (τ−½)/3), and the lattice velocity must stay well under the lattice speed of sound (0.577). Push the inlet speed up or τ down and the stability row will tell you before the picture does. Nothing here is smoothed to hide numerical trouble — a diverging run is reported as diverged.

A simulation that produces an attractive picture has demonstrated nothing. These are the checks that decide whether the solver is solving the right equations — run here, in the browser, on whichever backend is live, against answers that were known before the code was written.

Not run yet.

  1. CONSERVATION — a periodic box with no forcing. Streaming is a permutation and
     BGK collision preserves the first two moments, so mass and momentum must hold
     to round-off.

  2. POISEUILLE — force-driven flow between plates has an exact parabolic solution.
     A wrong viscosity, a wrong forcing prefactor or a misplaced wall all show up
     here and nowhere else.

  3. OBSTACLE FLOW — no closed form, so the check is that it stays bounded, keeps
     mass, and actually separates around the body.

  4. RESOLUTION SCALING — cells, steps/s and memory at each size.

The lattice is the physical representation, not a visualisation of something else. There are no particles: a moving mass is a pattern in the density and momentum fields, and it moves because those quantities are transported between neighbouring cells.

Architecture

Simulation
  ├── Lattice          dimensions, spacing, origin, indexing, topology
  ├── Fields           scalar / vector / tensor / distribution / material
  ├── Materials        per-cell classification (fluid, solid, inlet, outlet, moving)
  ├── PhysicsOperators declare the fields they read and write
  ├── Boundaries       regions applied in order over the lattice
  ├── Solver           ordering, ping-pong discipline, diagnostics
  └── Backends         WebGPU compute (production) · CPU reference (verification)

Indexing is index = x + Nx·(y + Ny·z) everywhere — JS, WGSL, boundaries, rendering. Fields are structure-of-arrays, so a component is contiguous across cells and adjacent GPU threads touch adjacent memory.

Why there are two backends

Per-cell physics belongs on the GPU and that is where it runs. But a solver that only executes on hardware the test environment lacks is a solver that ships unverified. This project's headless Chromium reaches WebGPU only behind an explicit flag, only on a secure origin, and only as a software adapter — good enough to prove a kernel correct, useless for throughput. So there is a CPU reference implementing the same equations from the same constants. It checks conservation and the Poiseuille profile against their analytic answers in plain Node on every test run; it is the fallback wherever WebGPU is absent; and where a GPU does exist, the two are run on the same scene and compared cell by cell. That parity check is not ceremony — it is what caught the production kernel emitting zeros, because macro is a reserved word in WGSL, every shader failed to compile, and WebGPU reports that asynchronously without throwing. The constants live in one module and the WGSL is generated from them, so a shader cannot quietly carry its own copy of 1/36.

What is deliberately not here yet

Heat, diffusion, elasticity, electromagnetics and multiphysics coupling are architecture, not code: an operator declares the fields it reads and writes, and the solver refuses two operators that write the same field in one stage, so coupling has to be stated rather than implied by call order. Adaptive resolution is not implemented either — but the lattice owns its own spacing and indexing rather than assuming unit cells, which is what keeps it possible.

The collision operator is BGK/SRT. Its known limitation is measured on the Verify tab: with a single relaxation time the effective wall position of bounce-back drifts with τ, exact only near τ = ½ + √(3/16) ≈ 0.933. That is what a TRT or MRT collision fixes, and it is why the operator takes a collision parameter instead of hard-coding one.
